Otella Speech + Swallow

Primary identity — one practice, two referral pathways

An earthy seventies palette and first-name warmth, carried on a Fraunces headline voice and a plain-language list of what a family is actually noticing. Two families only — Fraunces for anything with a voice, Nunito Sans for anything with a job — and the logotype is set in Fraunces too, so the wordmark and the name on the business card are the same letterforms.

The new constraint is that a parent and an ENT registrar have to trust the same page. Rather than splitting the difference into something bland, the identity carries two registers openly: warm wheat and gold for families, chocolate and chalk for clinicians, each with its own front door on the homepage.

Swallowing goes into the name, and the welcome swallow carries it in the mark. It signals the ENT, gastro and neuro pathway to the people who refer, and it reads perfectly plainly to a parent whose two-year-old is gagging on solids.

The inclusive position is carried as clinical service and as paperwork, not as a badge. Gender-affirming voice and communication training is a real speech pathology specialty with its own referral pathway, so it is listed as a service alongside everything else. The name and pronoun fields at the top of the letterhead do more day-to-day work than any statement would.

Chalk is the one addition to Route D’s palette, carried across from Route A’s chalk blue. It is the only cool colour in the system and it is reserved entirely for clinical material — referrer sections, report rules, scope statements. Warmth and coolness become the wayfinding, so a GP knows within a second which parts of a document were written for them.

Logo

16px and 24px

The supplied asset, dropped into every application. It is cropped by the roundel rather than sitting in a rounded square, which is the one thing worth changing from the reference — an app tile reads as software, a roundel reads as a stamp, and a stamp is what a solo practice actually needs. No text around the ring: the bird now fills the disc out to a thin double keyline, so the badge carries the mark alone and the name is always set beside it rather than bent around it. Hirundo neoxena, the welcome swallow, is local without resorting to a wave or a headland, and its common name is already what the door decal is trying to say.

One thing to decide. The asset is a seven-colour traced illustration, so it cannot invert and it cannot reduce. Two derived variants handle that and are shown here: a single-colour chocolate silhouette with the eye knocked out, for stamps, embroidery, foil and faxed correspondence; and for dark applications the mark keeps its own light disc rather than reversing, since inverting a painted bird produces a negative rather than a logo. The favicon and the 24px mark stay in full colour — the rust throat is what makes it read as this bird at that size, and dropping it just makes a dark blob. Both need to come out of the same source file when the artwork is finalised.

The crown in the asset is #11323B, a blue-black that sits outside the palette. On wheat it reads as a very dark cool and it is what the real bird looks like, so it is defensible — but it is the only cool dark in a system where chalk was meant to be the sole cool colour and to mean “this part is for clinicians”. Swapping the crown to chocolate is a one-line change if you want that distinction kept clean.

One shell, two audiences, on the same page. The chalk-ruled blocks are the clinical register and are written for the referrer; the gold panel is the family register and is capped at two items. A specialist reads the top two thirds and stops. A parent skips to the gold box.

The two chalk blocks that matter are the IDDSI diet and fluid level, and what she has referred onward. Both are safety items, and both are the things that get lost in a wall of prose.

Handwritten sign-off stays even on specialist correspondence. It costs nothing in credibility once the clinical structure above it is right, and it is the whole reason a family stays.

Name used and pronouns are the first row of the header, above name on record. That ordering is the whole policy: the name someone goes by is the name that appears in correspondence to a specialist, and the legal name sits in one field for billing.

Two body templates share this shell: full assessment (shown), and a one-page session note that drops the impression block and keeps the gold panel.

A handout is not a short letter, so it does not get the letterhead. The full lockup and the address block come off, and the header collapses to a 14mm band: mark, stacked wordmark at 9pt, and a topic pill naming which series the sheet belongs to. That buys back about a fifth of the page for content, which is the whole point.

Everything below it is the same furniture as the letterhead — gold practice box capped at two items, chalk rule for anything clinical, hand-signed voice — so a family that has both in the kitchen drawer reads them as one system.

The tick grid is what makes it a workbook rather than an information sheet. It is also the only honest way to find out whether the home programme actually happened, which is the single most useful thing to know at the next session.

Built as a .dotx with real named Word styles, not direct formatting — otherwise the numbering, the tables and any later table of contents come apart the first time someone edits it.

Rules for the template

  • ·Margins. 18 mm left and right, 12 mm top to the header band, 16 mm foot. Header band 14 mm; nothing else lives up there.
  • ·One accent per sheet. Gold for the thing to do, chalk for the thing to watch. Never both in the same block, and never a third colour.
  • ·It has to survive a photocopier. Every sheet is checked in greyscale at 90%. Colour never carries meaning on its own — the gold box also has a rule and a heading, the chalk note also has a label.
  • ·Two items, not twenty. The practice box is capped at two by the template, not by discipline. If a sheet needs more, it is two sheets.
  • ·Fonts will not be on the client’s machine. Fraunces and Nunito Sans are webfonts. Either embed them in the .dotx (File › Options › Save › Embed fonts) or set the substitution pair now — Georgia for Fraunces, Verdana for Nunito Sans — so a sheet opened on a school laptop still looks deliberate.
  • ·Named styles only. No direct formatting anywhere in the file. It is the difference between a template and a document someone once liked.

What I don’t do

No instrumental assessment. If a patient needs videofluoroscopy or FEES to answer the question, I will say so in the first letter and send them back to you rather than working around it for six weeks. Same for anything needing a laryngeal view before voice therapy, and for tracheostomy or ventilated patients.

How I work

Four things, and they’re the same whether you came from a specialist or found me on a noticeboard.

  1. You get me

    Same clinician every session, for as long as you’re coming. No rotating therapists, no handover when someone goes on leave.

  2. The first call is free

    Fifteen minutes on the phone. Some of those calls end with me saying you don’t need me yet.

  3. Two things, not twenty

    You leave each session with a short written note and two things to practise. Enough to matter, small enough to actually do.

  4. The goal is to finish

    I’ll tell you when we’re done, and I’ll tell your referrer why. I don’t keep people on the books because the slot is filled.

You won’t have to explain yourself

Some of this needs saying plainly, rather than being left for you to work out from a sticker on the door.

  • The name and pronouns you use go on my file, in my notes, and in every letter I write about you — whatever your Medicare card says. Your legal name sits in one field for billing and nowhere else.

  • Bring whoever you want into the room. Partner, parent, support worker, or a friend who does the talking on hard days.

The stated position sits after the four promises and before the personal section, in the warm register rather than the clinical one, because it is addressed to patients and not to referrers. The decal is now wordless — stripes, chevron and bird, no ring text — so it reads at a glance from the footpath and needs no reprint if the wording ever changes. It is deliberately a separate artefact from the logo: the mark stays clinical for correspondence, and the loud version goes on the door and the window, which is where people actually check before walking in.

The two doors sit directly under the hero, above everything else, and are the only place on the page where the palette flips cold. A parent and a registrar both land on the same URL and are sorted in one second without either being made to read the other’s copy. The service list then carries a small olive tag naming the pathway each item usually arrives by, which is a detail for referrers that costs a family nothing to ignore.
